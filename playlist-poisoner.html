The Playlist Poisoner Case

Posted: Friday, August 21, 2:30 AM

My own hearing is sensitive enough to track a slowing heartbeat from across a crowded room, so audio-based cases always come with a literal headache. The precinct was flooded with strange reports all week. Several unrelated individuals across the city were suffering sudden, severe panic attacks at exactly the same time of day. When I visited the victims in the triage ward, the scent of stale adrenaline and sheer terror was thick enough to choke on. The only connecting thread among them was digital: they had all downloaded a highly popular, anonymous instrumental lo-fi playlist, and were listening to the exact same track when the physiological symptoms hit.

I sat in the deepest, soundproofed basement of the lab with the volume turned down to a whisper, but my fangs still ached the moment the track played. To a human, a deep dive into the audio files revealed nothing unusual to the naked ear. However, I had the technicians run the tracks through an FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) analyzer to break the sound waves down into their individual frequencies. The spectrographic image—a visual representation of the audio spectrum—displayed a series of violent, high-pitched, staccato frequencies layered beneath the soothing basslines.

The hidden frequencies were maliciously calculated to perfectly match the resonant frequency of the human inner ear. It was causing immediate, severe vertigo and nausea, triggering the body's natural panic response. The anonymous artist behind the playlist wasn't a ghost or a demon; it was a disgruntled former audio engineer enacting revenge against a rival record label. By poisoning the viral playlist, he aimed to trigger a massive, financially ruinous recall of the label's newly released line of headphones, blaming their hardware for the illness. I found him hiding in a soundproofed studio downtown. He was smug until I crushed his favorite studio microphone into shrapnel with one hand. He confessed immediately after that.

Weekend Addendum: Tools of the Trade

Posted: Saturday, August 22, 11:15 PM

The heatwave has finally broken, leaving the city battered by a torrential, glorious thunderstorm. The complete absence of moonlight makes it the perfect evening to sit at my mahogany desk and write out the final incident reports for the audio engineer. Given how much time I spent staring at digital sound waves this week, I decided to completely disconnect from modern mechanics tonight and review a truly ancient writing instrument: the glass dip pen.

Unlike liquid ink pens or piston-filling fountain pens, the glass dip pen has no internal reservoir or moving parts. It is crafted from a single, solid piece of hand-spun glass. The magic lies entirely in the nib, which features a series of deep, twisted grooves spiraling down to a fine point. When you dip the pen into a bottle of ink, capillary action draws the liquid up into those grooves, holding it there against gravity. A single dip can write nearly a full page of notes. It forces you to slow down, to be deliberate and mindful of your strokes, which is a welcome change of pace from the frantic energy of the precinct.

The Verdict: The tactile feedback is entirely unique—it feels like writing with a very sharp, very smooth needle. Furthermore, cleaning it is incredibly simple; a quick rinse in a glass of water and the transparent grooves are pristine once again, ready for a completely different color of ink. While completely impractical for taking rapid notes out in the field, the glass dip pen is an absolute masterpiece for the quiet, undisturbed hours of the night.
